---
title: Why buy when you know you'll lose? Expected value, utility and prospect theory
description: A 1,000 KRW game has an expected value of 500 KRW, yet millions of people buy the lotto every week. We walk through what economics and psychology say, from Bernoulli's utility theory to Kahneman's prospect theory.
---

As worked out in the [post on odds and expected value](/blog/lotto-odds-explained.html), one 1,000 KRW lotto game has an expected value of about 500 KRW before tax. On average, you lose 500 KRW every time you buy. Even so, lotto sales in 2025 reached **6.2 trillion KRW**, the highest ever. Is it because people can't do the math? Economics and psychology give a different answer.

## Starting point: expected value

Expected value is the sum of "the value of each outcome × the probability of that outcome". In a game where heads pays 10,000 KRW and tails pays nothing, the expected value is 5,000 KRW. Going by expected value alone, paying more than 5,000 KRW to play this game is a loss.

If you judged by expected value alone, you shouldn't buy the lotto, and you shouldn't buy insurance either. An insurer can only stay in business if, on average, it pays out less than it collects in premiums. Yet buying insurance is seen as a sensible choice. So expected value alone can't explain the choices people make.

## Principle 1: Bernoulli's utility

In 1738 the mathematician Daniel Bernoulli gave a classic answer to this problem. What matters to people is not the **amount** of money but the **satisfaction (utility)** it brings. And the more money you have, the less satisfaction the same amount brings. 1m KRW weighs very differently for a poor person and for a rich one.

This theory explains insurance well. A big loss cuts utility sharply, so paying a small premium to avoid that risk is worth it. But the same theory struggles to explain the lotto. If utility diminishes, the appeal of a big prize should shrink just as much.

## Principle 2: Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory

In 1979 the psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky studied the choices real people make and published **prospect theory**. Kahneman won the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economics for this work. For the lotto, the key idea is **probability weighting**.

People do not feel probabilities as they really are.

| Actual probability | How much weight people give it |
|---|---|
| Very small (e.g. 1 in 8.15 million) | Felt as **larger** than it is |
| Medium (e.g. 50%) | Felt as slightly smaller than it is |
| Nearly certain (e.g. 99%) | Felt as far short of certain (100%) |

1 in 8.15 million is close to zero, but in people's minds it swells into "something that isn't zero". For the same reason, people gladly pay for insurance against very rare accidents. The lotto and insurance look like opposites, but both come from the same habit of **feeling small probabilities as large**.

## Principle 3: A week of daydreaming

Economists add one more explanation. The lotto sells not just an outcome but also **the anticipation while you wait**. For 1,000 KRW, you buy a week of imagining "what would I do if I won?" Seen this way, the expected value of −500 KRW becomes the price of an experience, like a movie ticket.

## How much is healthy?

Seeing it as the price of an experience only works when the amount is small. If you notice the following signs, you are buying losses, not an experience.

- You spend more to win back money you have lost.
- You buy with living expenses or borrowed money.
- Winning feels like the only way out of your money problems.

This is also why lotto purchases are limited to 100,000 KRW per draw. You can check again how small the odds for each prize rank really are in the table on the [lotto odds](/probability.html) page.
