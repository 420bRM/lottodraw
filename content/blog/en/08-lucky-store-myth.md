---
title: Do lucky stores really exist? Dividing 1st-prize wins by sales
description: Is a store that has sold dozens of 1st-prize tickets lucky, or did it simply sell a lot? We look at so-called lucky stores through base rates and survivorship bias.
---

Every Saturday there is a long queue outside stores with banners reading '1st prize sold XX times'. It is the same 1,000 KRW ticket, yet people travel a long way to buy it there because they believe the shop is a 'lucky store', the popular Korean term for a shop famous for selling winning tickets. What do lucky stores look like when you check the numbers?

## 1st prizes follow sales

Whichever store you buy from, the chance that one game wins 1st prize is 1 in 8,145,060. A store's terminal only records your numbers; it has no effect on the draw. So the expected number of 1st prizes a store sells is simple to work out.

> Expected 1st prizes = games sold by the store ÷ 8,145,060

On average, **one 1st prize comes up for about every 8.1 bn KRW of sales**. There are about 8,000 stores across the country, and busy ones sell tens of thousands of games a week. If such a store stays open for 20 years, its sales reach tens of billions of KRW, and selling several 1st prizes is in fact what you would expect. A lucky store is **a place that sells a lot, so more winners come from it**.

## A real comparison: 1st prizes per sales

In 2015, a newspaper divided the sales of well-known stores by the number of 1st prizes each had sold. A store in Nowon-gu, Seoul, famous for selling the most 1st prizes, had one 1st prize for about every **5.3 bn KRW** of sales, and a famous store in Busan had one for about every **2.5 bn KRW**. On the other hand, some small stores that had sold a few 1st prizes had one for about every 500m KRW. Measured against sales, the 'less famous' stores actually did better.

This does not mean those small stores are the real lucky stores. The reason lies in the following principles.

## Principle 1: survivorship bias

Imagine that all 8,000 stores sell lotto with exactly the same odds. By luck, some sell no 1st prizes, some sell one, and a very small number sell three or four. Those few hang banners and become lucky stores. Nobody remembers the thousands of stores that have never sold a 1st prize.

Looking for causes **only among the cases that survived** is an error called survivorship bias. A well-known example comes from World War II: the military wanted to reinforce the parts of returning bombers that had bullet holes, but the statistician Abraham Wald pointed out that "the planes that were hit where there are no bullet holes did not come back." A list of lucky stores is like a list made only of the bombers that returned.

## Principle 2: base rate neglect

You often see analyses like "30% of stores that sold a 1st prize are in large shopping districts in the Seoul area." But this figure only means something when compared with **how much of total sales is concentrated in those districts**. If 30% of sales come from there, it is no surprise that 30% of 1st prizes come from there too. Looking only at the result and leaving out the base rate you should compare it with is called **base rate neglect**.

## The feedback loop of the lucky store effect

A lucky store does not change the odds, but a reputation as a lucky store does change sales. Once word spreads, customers flock in; as sales go up, 1st prizes come up more often; and then the reputation grows further. A lucky store is not a cause but a result, and that result goes on to produce the next result.

## Summary

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Does buying at a lucky store raise the chance of winning? | No. It is the same wherever you buy. |
| Why do lucky stores sell so many 1st prizes? | Because they sell a lot of tickets. |
| What about stores that do well relative to their sales? | It is most likely the luck of a small sample. |

This site does not cover records by store, but you can see how the number of 1st-prize winners is spread across draws in [1st-prize winner statistics](/statistics-prize.html). The time and travel cost of going to a lucky store do not change the odds of winning. Still, if you enjoy the trip, that can be reason enough in itself. It is just better not to mix up the value of that enjoyment with the odds.

> The number of stores is about 8,000, based on 2026 reports. The sales-per-1st-prize comparison uses figures reported by Seoul Shinmun (31 July 2015). Before 2004 one game cost 2,000 KRW, so sales-based figures for older stores cannot be compared directly.
